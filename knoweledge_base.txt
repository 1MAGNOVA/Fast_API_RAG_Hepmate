### CHUNK C01 | Safety disclaimer (attach to every answer) | SRC: design | CONF: high | REFRESH: stable
This assistant gives general education only. It cannot diagnose, prescribe, interpret your personal
results as a final verdict, or replace a doctor. If you have symptoms, a positive test, or a possible
exposure, see a qualified clinician. In an emergency, go to the nearest hospital.

### CHUNK C02 | What hepatitis means | SRC: S01,S19,S20 | CONF: high | REFRESH: stable
Hepatitis means inflammation of the liver. Causes include the five hepatitis viruses (A, B, C, D, E),
heavy alcohol use, some medicines and supplements, toxins, and autoimmune disease. This knowledge base
focuses on viral hepatitis, with a short section on other causes (C26).

### CHUNK C03 | The five viruses at a glance | SRC: S01-S05 | CONF: high | REFRESH: stable
- A: contaminated food or water, or close contact. Short illness; no chronic form. Vaccine exists.
- B: blood, sexual contact, mother-to-baby, unsafe injections. Acute or chronic. Vaccine exists.
- C: mostly blood exposure. Often becomes chronic. No vaccine. Curable with antivirals.
- D: only infects people who have hepatitis B. Hepatitis B vaccination also prevents D.
- E: contaminated water (and some undercooked meat). Usually clears alone; dangerous in pregnancy.

### CHUNK C04 | Hepatitis A | SRC: S03,S10 | CONF: high | REFRESH: stable
Spread by swallowing the virus from contaminated food, water, or hands, or by close contact. Risk tracks
with poor sanitation and unsafe water. Nearly everyone recovers fully and gains lifelong immunity; a very
small number develop sudden severe liver failure. There is no specific antiviral; care is supportive
(rest, fluids, nutrition, no alcohol). A safe, effective vaccine exists; WHO supports it in national
programs depending on local burden, and a single dose is used to control outbreaks.

### CHUNK C05 | Hepatitis B - how it spreads | SRC: S01,S07 | CONF: high | REFRESH: stable
Spread by infected blood and body fluids: mother to baby at birth, in early childhood, sex with an infected
partner, unsafe injections, and shared sharp instruments. It is NOT spread by hugging, coughing, sharing
plates or cups, or casual contact. Infection cannot be told apart from other viral hepatitis by symptoms,
so lab testing is required.

### CHUNK C06 | Hepatitis B - acute vs chronic | SRC: S01,S07 | CONF: med | REFRESH: yearly
Most healthy adults clear a new infection. The younger the person at infection, the higher the chance it
becomes chronic: WHO's long-standing figures say under 5% of adult infections but about 95% of infant
infections become chronic (check the current WHO wording before quoting numbers). Chronic infection is
defined by the virus surface antigen staying positive for longer than 6 months. Many chronic carriers feel
fine for years while liver damage builds silently.

### CHUNK C07 | Hepatitis B - treatment overview | SRC: S01,S07,S22,S23 | CONF: med | REFRESH: yearly
Acute hepatitis B: supportive care only. Chronic hepatitis B: daily oral antivirals such as tenofovir or
entecavir that suppress the virus and protect the liver; most people who start must continue long term and
need regular monitoring. Treatment decisions use blood tests (liver enzymes, viral load, markers) and
scans. WHO 2024 simplified and widened who qualifies for treatment, including adolescents. Societies differ
on details such as when stopping is allowed (see C30), so decisions belong to a specialist.

### CHUNK C08 | Hepatitis B - what the blood markers mean | SRC: S17,S01 | CONF: med | REFRESH: stable
HBsAg = virus currently present. anti-HBs = protective antibody (from vaccine or recovered infection).
anti-HBc (total) = past or present exposure. General patterns: HBsAg positive = infected (needs follow-up);
anti-HBs positive only = immune by vaccination; anti-HBs and anti-HBc positive with HBsAg negative =
recovered and immune; all three negative = never infected and not protected (vaccination advised).
Educational only - a clinician must interpret any individual result, and the pattern with core antibody
alone needs expert review.

### CHUNK C09 | Hepatitis B - screening and vaccination (US CDC) | SRC: S17,S24-note | CONF: med | REFRESH: yearly
CDC recommends everyone aged 18 or older be screened at least once with the three-test panel (HBsAg,
anti-HBs, total anti-HBc), screening in every pregnancy (ideally first trimester), and hepatitis B
vaccination for all adults aged 19-59 (older adults with risk factors or who ask). Other countries differ;
follow local guidance.

### CHUNK C10 | Hepatitis B vaccine | SRC: S01 | CONF: high | REFRESH: stable
Safe and effective. WHO advises every baby get a first dose as soon as possible after birth (within 24
hours), then follow the national schedule. WHO says protection lasts at least 20 years and probably for
life. Health workers and people at risk should be vaccinated. Vaccination also prevents hepatitis D.

### CHUNK C11 | Hepatitis B in pregnancy and newborns | SRC: S01,S07,S12,S23 | CONF: med | REFRESH: yearly
Most chronic infections worldwide come from mother-to-baby spread. Pregnant women should be screened.
The baby should get the vaccine within 24 hours of birth, and sometimes an antibody injection (HBIG).
WHO 2024 widened eligibility for antiviral prophylaxis in pregnancy; AASLD/IDSA 2025 describes starting
tenofovir (TDF or TAF) around week 28 when the mother's viral load is high. Only the maternity team can
decide this for a given patient.

### CHUNK C12 | Hepatitis C basics | SRC: S02 | CONF: high | REFRESH: yearly
A blood-borne virus. Most infections come from unsafe injections or procedures in health care, unscreened
transfusions, sharing needles or syringes, and sexual practices that expose blood. It can cause acute and
chronic hepatitis, from mild to lifelong disease including cirrhosis and liver cancer. Many people have no
symptoms for years. No vaccine exists.

### CHUNK C13 | Hepatitis C testing | SRC: S15,S16,S02 | CONF: high | REFRESH: stable
Two steps: (1) antibody test shows past or present exposure; (2) if reactive, a test for viral RNA shows
whether the virus is still there. Many labs now run RNA automatically on every reactive antibody result
to avoid missed follow-up. Antibody positive plus RNA positive means current infection; antibody positive
plus RNA negative usually means past infection that cleared (a clinician confirms).

### CHUNK C14 | Hepatitis C treatment and cure | SRC: S02,S09,S24 | CONF: high | REFRESH: yearly
Direct-acting antiviral tablets cure more than 95% of people. Courses are short - commonly 8 to 12 weeks,
and sometimes up to 24 weeks, depending on liver scarring and other factors. WHO recommends these
pan-genotype regimens for adults, adolescents, and children from age 3 with chronic infection. Cure does not
give lasting immunity: reinfection is possible if exposure continues. Access to diagnosis and treatment is
still low in many places, so ask about national programs.

### CHUNK C15 | Hepatitis C screening (US CDC) | SRC: S16 | CONF: med | REFRESH: yearly
CDC recommends screening all adults 18 and older at least once and every pregnancy (unless local
prevalence is very low), plus anyone with ongoing risk. Other countries use risk-based or national
policies; follow local guidance.

### CHUNK C16 | Hepatitis D | SRC: S04,S30 | CONF: med | REFRESH: yearly
Needs hepatitis B to exist, so only people with hepatitis B can get it (at the same time or later). It is
the most severe form of viral hepatitis and speeds up cirrhosis and liver cancer. Roughly 1 in 20 people
with hepatitis B are also infected. Hepatitis B antivirals do not treat D itself. Bulevirtide (Hepcludex)
was approved in Europe in 2020; options are limited and specialist-led. Anyone with hepatitis B should ask
about a hepatitis D test. WHO 2024 added testing guidance. Prevention: hepatitis B vaccination.

### CHUNK C17 | Hepatitis E | SRC: S05,S18 | CONF: high | REFRESH: stable
Spread by contaminated drinking water (outbreaks after floods or in camps) and sometimes undercooked
animal meat. Usually a short illness that clears on its own. It can be severe or fatal in pregnant women,
especially later in pregnancy, and can become chronic in people with weakened immunity. No specific cure;
care is supportive. Prevention: safe water, sanitation, food hygiene.

### CHUNK C18 | Hepatitis E vaccine | SRC: S05,S11,S18 | CONF: med | REFRESH: yearly
One vaccine (Hecolin) is licensed in China and a few other places, not available in most countries.
WHO's vaccine-safety committee reviewed its use in pregnancy after a Bangladesh trial hinted at more early
miscarriages in women vaccinated around the start of pregnancy, while stressing that the disease itself is
dangerous in pregnancy. Decisions need local public health advice.

### CHUNK C19 | Common symptoms | SRC: S01,S19 | CONF: high | REFRESH: stable
Many people have none. When they appear: tiredness, nausea, poor appetite, upper-right belly discomfort,
yellow skin or eyes (jaundice), dark urine, pale stools, fever, joint aches. Symptoms cannot tell the
virus type apart; only blood tests can.

### CHUNK C20 | Emergency warning signs | SRC: general clinical practice (verify with S14) | CONF: med | REFRESH: stable
Go to urgent care or an emergency room for: confusion, unusual sleepiness, or behavior change; vomiting
blood or black stools; persistent vomiting; a swollen painful belly; severe jaundice with weakness; easy
bleeding or bruising; or any liver symptoms while pregnant. These can signal serious liver injury.

### CHUNK C21 | Who should be tested | SRC: S02,S17,S16,S27 | CONF: med | REFRESH: yearly
Pregnant women; anyone who has shared needles or had unsafe injections, procedures, or transfusions;
household or sexual partners of someone with hepatitis B or C; health workers; people living with HIV;
people from high-prevalence regions; babies born to infected mothers; anyone with unexplained abnormal
liver tests. Several countries recommend at least one lifetime screen for all adults - check local rules.

### CHUNK C22 | Preventing spread day to day | SRC: S01,S02,S14 | CONF: high | REFRESH: stable
Do not share razors, toothbrushes, nail tools, needles, or syringes. Cover cuts. Clean blood spills with
gloves and a bleach solution. Use condoms with partners who are not vaccinated or immune to hepatitis B.
Get vaccinated. Wash hands and use safe water and food. Use only sterile equipment for injections,
piercing, tattooing, and dental work.

### CHUNK C23 | Common myths | SRC: S01,S02 | CONF: high | REFRESH: stable
Myth: you catch hepatitis B or C from hugging, sharing meals, or sitting together. Fact: no. Myth: no
symptoms means no problem. Fact: damage can be silent. Myth: hepatitis B can be cured with herbs or
supplements. Fact: no herbal product is proven to cure it, and some supplements damage the liver (C24).

### CHUNK C24 | Living with chronic hepatitis - daily habits | SRC: S19,S21,S07 | CONF: med | REFRESH: yearly
Avoid alcohol. Keep every follow-up and lab visit. Take medicine exactly as prescribed and do not stop
without your specialist - stopping hepatitis B antivirals can cause a flare. Ask a doctor or pharmacist
before any new medicine, herbal remedy, or supplement. Acetaminophen (paracetamol) is safe at normal
doses for most people but high or repeated doses can seriously injure the liver, especially with alcohol;
ask a clinician what limit is right for you. Stay vaccinated against hepatitis A and B if not immune.

### CHUNK C25 | Complications and monitoring | SRC: S01,S07,S22 | CONF: med | REFRESH: yearly
Long-term hepatitis B or C can cause scarring (cirrhosis), liver failure, and liver cancer. Regular blood
tests and scans measure liver health; some patients need periodic ultrasound for cancer surveillance.
Treatment greatly lowers these risks. Ask your specialist which schedule fits you.

### CHUNK C26 | Other causes of hepatitis | SRC: S19,S20,S21 | CONF: med | REFRESH: stable
Alcohol-related liver disease, medicine- or supplement-induced injury (thousands of products are listed in
the LiverTox database), autoimmune hepatitis, and fatty liver disease can all inflame the liver and need
different care from viral hepatitis. A doctor works out which cause applies.

### CHUNK C27 | Co-infection with HIV, B and C | SRC: S02,S07,S23 | CONF: med | REFRESH: yearly
HIV, hepatitis B, hepatitis C, and hepatitis D can occur together and change both testing and drug choices.
People with HIV should be tested for hepatitis B and C. Never change antivirals on your own; the HIV and
liver teams must coordinate.

### CHUNK C28 | Nigeria context (include if your users are in Nigeria) | SRC: S27,S28 | CONF: med | REFRESH: yearly
Nigeria is endemic for both hepatitis B and C. The 2018 national AIDS indicator survey (adults 15-64)
reported about 8.1% for hepatitis B and 1.1% for hepatitis C; an earlier 2013 survey had given about 11%
and 2.2%. The Federal Ministry of Health published its first national viral hepatitis guidelines in 2016
and a strategic plan for 2016-2020. Hepatitis B vaccination is in the national childhood schedule
(birth dose and pentavalent); the Minister cited birth-dose coverage of 59% in 2019. A policy exists to
screen pregnant women for HIV, syphilis, and viral hepatitis. Figures are dated - refresh from current
ministry or WHO releases before quoting.

### CHUNK C29 | Global burden (dated figures) | SRC: S01,S02 | CONF: med | REFRESH: yearly
WHO 2026 fact sheets estimate about 240 million people living with chronic hepatitis B and 47 million with
chronic hepatitis C in 2024, with around 1.1 million hepatitis B deaths and 239,000 hepatitis C deaths that
year, mostly from cirrhosis and liver cancer. Other publications give different totals (for example, 254-296
million for hepatitis B) because of different years and methods. Always say "estimates" and cite the year.

### CHUNK C30 | When guidelines disagree | SRC: S07,S22,S23 | CONF: high | REFRESH: yearly
WHO (built for settings with limited labs), EASL (Europe), and AASLD/IDSA (US) differ on points such as
treatment thresholds and whether antivirals can ever be stopped. EASL 2025 allows considering stopping in
selected patients with long viral suppression; AASLD/IDSA 2025 conditionally advises against stopping
until the surface antigen is lost. The bot should present this as "experts differ" and send the person to
their own specialist, never pick a side.

### CHUNK C31 | Emotional side and stigma | SRC: general | CONF: med | REFRESH: stable
A diagnosis can feel frightening or isolating. Hepatitis B and C are common and manageable, and many
people live full lives with proper care. Encourage talking with a clinician, counselor, or patient support
group, and sharing the diagnosis only as comfortable. Do not judge how someone got infected.

### CHUNK C32 | Questions to take to the doctor | SRC: general | CONF: high | REFRESH: stable
Which type do I have and is it acute or chronic? Do I need treatment now, and what are the options?
How often should I have blood tests and scans? Do my family and partners need testing or vaccines?
Which medicines, supplements, and painkillers are safe for me? What symptoms mean I should come in at once?

### CHUNK C33 | Chatbot rules (copy into the system prompt) | SRC: design | CONF: high | REFRESH: stable
- Answer only from retrieved chunks; if context is missing or weak, say so and advise a clinician.
- Cite the source ID/name for each answer; show the data year for statistics.
- Never diagnose, never give personal drug doses, never tell anyone to start or stop a medicine.
- Never interpret a user's own lab numbers as a final verdict; explain what markers mean in general.
- Escalate emergency signs (C20) immediately, before anything else.
- Avoid blame; keep a calm, plain-language tone; ask only one follow-up question.
- Mention that guidelines differ by country when relevant (C30).

### CHUNK C34 | Out-of-scope and refusal templates | SRC: design | CONF: high | REFRESH: stable
- "Can you diagnose me?" -> explain you cannot, describe what testing involves, suggest seeing a clinician.
- "What dose of X should I take?" -> decline; recommend the prescriber or pharmacist.
- "Stop my medicine?" -> advise not to stop without the specialist; explain why briefly.
- Questions about unrelated diseases -> say this advisor is limited to hepatitis and liver health.
- Signs of crisis (self-harm, severe distress) -> respond with care and direct to local emergency/crisis help.
